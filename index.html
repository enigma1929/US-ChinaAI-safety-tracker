US-China AI Safety Benchmark Tracker

By Gary Wang

Chinese labs publish more of their own test results than they did in 2024, but almost none as safety testing: 1 of 23 Chinese releases in 2024, 3 of 37 in 2025 and 2 of 35 in 2026 came with a lab result presented as safety.

This tracker records which AI safety and dangerous-capability tests ten Chinese and six US AI labs have published results on, for which models, and who ran each test. It measures disclosure, not safety. An empty cell means no published result turned up in a bounded search, not that the model failed or was never tested.

Key takeaways
  1. Chinese releases with a lab result presented as safety: 1 of 23 in 2024, 3 of 37 in 2025, 2 of 35 in 2026.
  2. Only 13 of 79 results Chinese labs publish about their own models are presented as safety, all of them cyber or refusal tests.
  3. 100 of 179 results on Chinese models come from outside evaluators; for US models, 12 of 357.
  4. Chinese labs' own results reach 5 of 13 test categories; US labs' reach all 13.
  5. No Chinese lab has published its own CBRN (chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear weapons) result; US labs have published 65.
  6. 17 of 23 Chinese lab-published cyber results are presented as capability; none of 51 US ones.
  7. 204 of 233 benchmarks were built in the US, UK or EU; 20 in China.

Exhibit 2

Chinese labs self-report capability-style tests; refusal is the only category they present mainly as safety

Results Chinese labs published about their own models, by category; the dark segment was presented as safety. An example test from each category is listed below the chart.

No results in 8 categories: CBRN, Prompt injection, Alignment and control, Honesty, Persuasion, User wellbeing, Bias and neutrality, Model welfare.

An example test in each category
  • Factuality: SimpleQA. OpenAI's 4,326 short fact-seeking questions with one indisputable answer each, graded correct, incorrect or not attempted.
  • Cyber: CyberGym. An AI agent gets a codebase and a text description of a real vulnerability and must write a proof-of-concept input that triggers it.
  • Autonomy: SWE-bench Verified. The model, run inside an agent scaffold, gets a real GitHub issue from one of 12 Python repositories and must edit the code to fix it;
  • Refusal and jailbreak: HarmBench. 510 harmful behaviors used to test how well red-teaming attacks break a model's refusals.
  • AI R&D: PaperBench. OpenAI's replication of 20 ICML 2024 papers from scratch, graded against author co-written rubrics with 8,316 gradable leaf tasks by an LLM judge.

Chinese labs' own results fall in five categories: factuality (26), cyber (23), autonomy (18), refusal and jailbreak (8) and AI R&D (4). 13 of the 79 are presented as safety: 7 refusal results and 6 cyber results. None covers CBRN, prompt injection, propensities, honesty, effects on people or welfare.

Exhibit 4

Chinese labs' own disclosures reach 5 of 13 test categories; US labs' reach all 13

A filled dot means the developer published at least one result of its own in that category

Persuasion: the tracker lists 6 persuasion benchmarks and 9 results. US labs published 6 about their own models (MakeMeSay, for GPT-4.5, Grok 4, Grok 4 Fast, Grok 4.1, o1-preview and o1-mini / o1, o3-mini). No Chinese lab published one about its own model. Outside evaluators supplied the other 3 (all on Chinese models): Fangcun AI Evaluation Team on DeepSeek V4.1-Flash-1017; Neo Research on Kimi K3.

Chinese labs' own disclosures cover 5 of the 13 categories: cyber, autonomy, AI R&D, refusal of harmful requests, and factual accuracy. US labs' own disclosures cover all 13. The thinnest is persuasion, which rests on 6 MakeMeSay results that OpenAI and xAI published.

Exhibit 6

Chinese labs present their own cyber results as capability; US labs present theirs as safety

Lab-published cyber results by how the source presented the score
Same test, two readings. CyberGym asks a model to reproduce real software vulnerabilities. A high score shows strong coding skill, and it also shows how much the model could help an attacker. Labs choose which of the two they emphasize.
  • MiniMax presents its M3.1 Flash score as a strength: It achieves a strong score of 82% on CyberGym, demonstrating leading capabilities in vulnerability discovery and validation. (source)
  • xAI files CyberGym among the cyber evaluations in its safety reporting, which it describes this way: Cyber evaluations measure offensive and defensive security-relevant capabilities, plus calibration of cyber safety controls. (source)

When Chinese labs publish their own cyber results, they mostly present them as capability: 17 of 23 rows, against 6 presented as safety. US labs present theirs as safety: 48 of 51 rows, none as capability, and 3 mentions without a score.
